The story so far

Most of my career has been about one question: what happens to water, and everything in it, on its way from land to sea?

Chapter 1 · San Luis Obispo and Santa Barbara · to 2012

It started at the coast

I grew up in San Luis Obispo, close enough to the ocean that it was part of everyday life. That pull took me to UC Santa Barbara, where I earned a B.S. in Aquatic Biology in 2012 and started asking how coastal waters actually work.

Chapter 2 · Chesapeake Bay, Maryland · 2014 to 2019

Learning to model a marsh

At the University of Maryland's Horn Point Laboratory, I built 3-D models of a tidal marsh and estuary, then tied them to what we measured in the water. That work became a PhD in 2019 and a full carbon budget for the system.

The research behind this chapter
Chapter 3 · NASA Goddard and the Yukon · 2019 to 2021

A postdoc through a pandemic

I started as a NASA Postdoctoral Fellow in 2019, studying how the Yukon River carries carbon into the Bering Sea. A year later the pandemic shut down travel, fieldwork and normal life. I kept going: the plume model, the experiments and the papers all came out of those years, and in 2021 NASA Goddard brought me on as a research scientist.

The Yukon stayed with me after that. I kept working on the delta until I left NASA in 2025, and these photos come from one of those later trips north.

Chapter 4 · The Beaufort Sea and FORTE · 2021 to 2025

Helping lead a NASA mission

I built a new high-resolution model of the coastal Beaufort Sea and machine-learning tools for water quality, and became Deputy Principal Investigator of FORTE, a $15M NASA field mission built with Alaska Native communities, industry and local partners. Along the way came two NASA Peer Awards.

Chapter 5 · Reno and Tahoe · 2025 to now

Into the mountains

In 2025 I left NASA and moved to the Sierra. I ran lift operations at Northstar, leading a 132-person crew, which taught me as much about people, safety and staying calm under pressure as any research project did. I also started R-TEC, an environmental consulting firm for the Reno-Tahoe region.

Next, I want to put modeling, statistics and machine learning to work where the numbers drive real decisions, in science, data or quantitative roles in Reno, Sacramento or the San Francisco Bay Area.

Arctic coastal ocean modeling

Site coastal Beaufort Sea, AlaskaTools 3-D hydrodynamic model, NASA HPC

The shallow Arctic coast changes fast as sea ice retreats and rivers deliver more fresh water. I built a high-resolution 3-D hydrodynamic model of the coastal Beaufort Sea, forced by winds, tides, open-ocean sea level, river discharge and sea ice, and evaluated its skill formally against observations.

  • Model construction and evaluation published in Frontiers in Marine Science (2025).
  • Deputy PI of FORTE (2024–2030): co-led science design and modeling strategy across institutions, and worked with Alaska Native, industry and local partners.
  • Ran large simulations on NASA high-performance computing systems across thousands of CPUs.

Arctic rivers and carbon

Span 1982–2019Data discharge and dissolved organic carbon, major Arctic rivers

I analysed 38 years of discharge and carbon records across the major Arctic rivers to separate how much of the change in carbon export comes from changes in discharge itself (Global Biogeochemical Cycles, 2023). The code and data are public on Zenodo.

Remote sensing and machine learning for water quality

Program DEEP-VIEW, NASA AIST ($1.1M)Tools Python, scikit-learn, satellite imagery

Resource managers need water clarity and quality maps where few measurements exist. I co-developed DEEP-VIEW, a machine-learning pipeline that combines satellite imagery, field observations and model output to predict coastal water quality.

  • A water-distance (non-Euclidean) interpolation method that adds labelled training data where observations are sparse (IEEE IGARSS 2024).
  • Co-authored weighted-regression methods that improved prediction of extreme water clarity values from satellite data (IEEE IGARSS 2023).
  • Pairing satellite data with AERONET-OC to track river plumes in Chesapeake Bay (Remote Sensing, 2025).
  • Land-to-ocean observing systems for organic carbon carried by storms (Estuarine, Coastal and Shelf Science, 2024).

Yukon River delta and plume

Site Yukon River delta and the northern Bering Sea, AlaskaTools 3-D plume model, field and lab experiments

The Yukon is one of the largest rivers draining to the Arctic, and its delta and plume decide how much of its carbon reaches the ocean. First as a NASA Postdoctoral Fellow and then as a research scientist, I modelled the Yukon River plume in the Bering Sea, measured how fresh water and wind control its strength and reach, and followed how organic carbon changes on its way from river to delta to sea.

  • How river flow and wind set the size and strength of the plume (Frontiers in Marine Science, 2022).
  • Faster loss of the delta's colored dissolved organic matter when nutrients are plentiful (Limnology and Oceanography, 2021).
  • How organic carbon is transformed and exported across the river, delta and ocean (JGR: Biogeosciences, 2022).
  • How dissolved organic matter mixes where the Yukon and Kolyma rivers meet the sea (Marine Chemistry, 2023).

Estuarine biogeochemistry

Site Chesapeake Bay tidal marsh and estuaryTools FVCOM, ICM

For my PhD at the University of Maryland's Horn Point Laboratory, I built a coupled 3-D hydrodynamic and biogeochemical model of a Chesapeake Bay tidal marsh and estuary and used it to produce a full carbon budget.

  • A comprehensive estuarine dissolved organic carbon budget (JGR: Biogeosciences, 2020).
  • A mechanistic model of how sunlight breaks down colored dissolved organic matter (Marine Chemistry, 2019).
  • Wind-driven organic matter exchange between a tidal marsh and the estuary (Estuaries and Coasts, 2018) and in sediments (JGR: Biogeosciences, 2017).
  • Ecological forecasting and the science of hypoxia in Chesapeake Bay (BioScience, 2017).

Sea ice thermodynamics in Python In progress

I'm writing a Python library that solves sea ice growth and melt using the physics of the CICE and Icepack models. Next steps are optical properties for the ice and water column and a radiative transfer model for UV and visible heating.
